Truth Has a Material Address

Where data comes from, and why it matters for teaching physics after AI
Alessio Bellino
Pontificia Universidad Católica de Chile
A computer scientist, not a physicist

I work on the materiality of learning.

My question was simple: does hands-on learning work because of the equipment—or because of the material experience itself?
I think it's the experience.
Then it struck me

Truths come from data—and from the material experience that makes them.

So the same idea I study for learning might help with something bigger: teaching truth in the age of AI.
The problem

It's getting hard to know what's true.

Information arrives already packaged. And AI can now produce convincing data—graphs, whole "experiments"—that were never measured.
Physics has a good answer

Go and measure the world yourself.

A result you can trust is not one that looks real—it's one you can trace back to a real measurement.
Computing has a word for that trace

Provenance.

The history of a number: where it came from, and whether anyone can check it.
Two graphs can look identical
Where fake evidence enters
1

in how the data are processed

2

in whether there was ever a measurement at all

This talk takes the second—the link back to the world.
So change the question we teach
"Is this real?"
"Where does this come from—can I follow it back to the world, or only to a prompt and an AI model?"
In the classroom

Build the chain yourself.

Program an ordinary phone into a measuring instrument: world → sensor → number → graph, and open every step.
Ready-made experiments already live in phyphox—g, a pendulum's period, the apparent forces on a moving train.
A caveat

The phone isn't the point.

It's a black box—so the real work is opening it: writing the code decides how the raw signal becomes a number. A string, a weight, a stopwatch do the same job. The phone just happens to be in every pocket.
The tool matters far less than the act.
What I can't prove yet

Does building honest data help students spot dishonest data?

I believe so. I can't prove it yet. That's the reason I'm here.
Questions for the room
  1. Is it useful to teach that something is "true" when you can trace it back to a real measurement—or does that leave too much out?
  2. Physics already does this well: a real result can be repeated and traced back to how it was made. AI content can look the same but has no such trail. How do we help students tell them apart?
  3. If the cure is checking things yourself, everyone needs to be able to do it—yet the phone is also where most fake content arrives. Can the same device be both the problem and the cure?
